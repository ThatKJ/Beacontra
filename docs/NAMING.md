# Naming: Candidate Universe & First Elimination

**Status:** Phase 1 (generation) + Phase 2 (first elimination) complete — CLAUDE. Phase 3 (collision audit) is GEMINI's, independent, in parallel — do not wait for this document to be "finished" before starting it. OPENCODE: do not rename anything until `docs/NAMING_DECISION.md` exists with `NAMING STATUS: FINAL`.

**Brief, restated so the bar is explicit:** the product lets a brand owner submit a product name + reference photo; the system finds live marketplace listings (`google_shopping`/`amazon_product`) and cross-checks them against the reference photo (`google_lens`), fusing price/seller/visual signals into a ranked review queue. It does not assert counterfeit status — it surfaces commercial anomalies and brand-risk signals for human review. The name needs to survive being said out loud in a 3-minute demo, read on a GitHub repo, and not sound like it was generated by mechanically gluing two nouns together. Explicitly avoiding, per direct instruction: `Guard`, `Secure`, `Detect`, `Vision`, `Lens`, `Smart`, `Intelli`, anything ending in `AI` unless exceptional, and accusatory names (`CounterfeitDetector`, `FakeFinder`, `FraudHunter`) or their near-variants (`BrandGuard`, `BrandWatch AI`, `CounterfeitAI`, `ProductLens`, `VerifyAI`).

## Phase 1 — 30 candidates across 5 categories

### A. Professional / B2B

**1. Provenant**
- Pronunciation: proh-VEN-ant
- Meaning: derived from "provenance" — proof of origin/authenticity lineage.
- Why it fits: the product's whole job is tracing whether a listing's evidence lines up with the genuine article's provenance. The word itself already means almost exactly this.
- Negative interpretation: none obvious; unusual enough that it reads as invented, not literal.
- Memorability: high — unusual, rhythmic, no direct English-dictionary competitor.
- Demo sound: strong — "Provenant found three listings worth reviewing" sounds like a real product sentence.

**2. Marqline**
- Pronunciation: MARK-line
- Meaning: "Marq" (brand/mark) + "line" (a brand's product line, or a line of evidence).
- Why it fits: directly evokes tracking a brand's product line across the market.
- Negative interpretation: "Marq-" prefix is a fairly common fintech/brand-tool naming pattern (feels close to a cluster of similar-sounding startups) — worth a collision check specifically on this prefix.
- Memorability: medium.
- Demo sound: decent, slightly generic-SaaS cadence.

**3. Trueshelf**
- Pronunciation: TROO-shelf
- Meaning: "true" + "shelf" (a marketplace shelf/listing).
- Why it fits: retail-specific, human, not accusatory — "is this really on the shelf as advertised."
- Negative interpretation: "shelf" could read as physical-retail-only, less obviously about online marketplaces at first glance.
- Memorability: medium-high, warm/friendly tone.
- Demo sound: good, easy to say naturally.

**4. Verolist**
- Pronunciation: VEH-roh-list
- Meaning: "vero" (Latin/Italian "true") + "list" (listing).
- Why it fits: directly names the core object (a listing) plus the core action (verification).
- Negative interpretation: "Vero" is also an actual photo-sharing social app's name (Vero True Social) — real collision risk, flag strongly for Gemini.
- Memorability: medium.
- Demo sound: fine, slightly clinical.

**5. Attestly**
- Pronunciation: uh-TEST-lee
- Meaning: from "attest" (to bear witness/confirm) + brandable "-ly" suffix.
- Why it fits: the product attests to evidence, not to a verdict — matches the "evidence, not accusation" positioning well.
- Negative interpretation: the "-ly" suffix is a very well-worn SaaS-naming pattern (Grammarly, Calendly, Loomly) — risks sounding like a template name despite the meaning fitting well.
- Memorability: medium — the suffix pattern is so common it may not stick.
- Demo sound: fine.

**6. Marketflag**
- Pronunciation: MAR-ket-flag
- Meaning: literal — flags something in the market.
- Why it fits: plain, immediately understandable.
- Negative interpretation: functionally descriptive rather than brandable — reads more like a feature name than a company name; also close to generic phrase "flag in the market."
- Memorability: low — too literal to stand out.
- Demo sound: fine but forgettable.

## Phase 5 decision rubric — prepared in advance, before Gemini's evidence lands

Writing this now, not after seeing the collision audit, so the final call is applied consistently rather than rationalized after the fact. When `docs/NAMING_AUDIT.md` and Gemini's red-team narrow the field to a top 3, I'll weigh:

1. **Collision risk (gating, not just scoring)** — a SIGNIFICANT or REJECT-level collision (per Gemini's classification) eliminates a name outright, regardless of how well it scores elsewhere. A MINOR collision is a real but survivable mark against it, not disqualifying on its own.
2. **Product relevance** — does the name's meaning (where it has one) actually describe what this product does, not just sound nice? Names like Halcy/Nuvira were already cut in Phase 2 on this basis; the same standard applies going into Phase 5.
3. **Pronounceability/demo quality** — can a presenter say it naturally mid-sentence three times in a 3-minute demo without stumbling or sounding like they're announcing a product for the first time?
4. **Memorability** — would a judge who saw 15 other demos that day still recall the name the next morning?
5. **Future extensibility** — does the name box the product into "reverse-image verification only," or would it still make sense if price/seller signals became the dominant feature, or if the product expanded to other evidence types later? (This is explicitly why literal-mechanism names like Pixeltrace/Frametrace scored lower in Phase 2 than metaphor-based ones like Mirrorcheck/Echomark.)
6. **Brand tone match** — does it sound like a serious tool for a brand owner dealing with a real financial problem, not a consumer app or a security-vendor cliché?

**Tie-breaking rule:** if two names are genuinely indistinguishable after applying all six criteria to the actual evidence (not a coin flip disguised as analysis), I'll say so explicitly rather than force a false-confidence pick — but the default expectation, per the mission, is that evidence resolves this without needing to ask the user to choose.
